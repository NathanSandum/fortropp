# Production (item 16): questions to settle

Written 2026-10-19 as things to think about before starting item 16. These are questions, not research results: prices and plan details must be checked against Cloudflare's current docs when the work starts.

## 1. Apex domains

A CNAME can't sit at the root of a domain, so where does `a.com` (not `www.a.com`) point?
- The customer's DNS provider supports ALIAS / CNAME flattening, pointing at our edge name.
- Cloudflare's apex proxying (fixed IPs the customer points A records at; a paid add-on, from memory).
- A product rule: the apex only redirects to `www`.

Shapes the instructions in the domains UI.

Already settled in item 5 and built: ALIAS at the apex, CNAME below it, never A records (`backend/src/models/domain.rs:131` sends `record_type: "ALIAS"` for apexes; the setup step shows it). Open, small: the explainer (`DnsExplainer.svelte`) says nothing about providers without ALIAS/flattening support (e.g. GoDaddy); whether and how to guide those users (registrar forwarding to `www`, or moving DNS to Cloudflare) is a content decision for the domain UI work.

2026-10-22: no redesign for non-ALIAS providers until HTTPS-record AliasMode (RFC 9460) is verified for real (§9). GoDaddy pushes it as its answer ("do everything CNAME flattening does", Managed DNS, 2026-09-03 blog) while advising A/AAAA alongside for clients and resolvers that don't pass HTTPS records. Fixed-IP fallbacks considered: Cloudflare's apex proxying is sales-gated with static IP prefixes at extra cost; Cloudflare's own proxied IPs aren't promised to stay put; our own reserved IP + redirector puts serving on our machine. Registrar survey (2026-10, top registrars by domains): ALIAS/ANAME at Namecheap, Squarespace (DNSSEC off), Dynadot (ANAME), Hostinger, Porkbun, Gandi, Cloudflare; none at GoDaddy (HTTPS records only), NameSilo (root forwarding instead), Network Solutions, IONOS (undocumented); Wix offers only A records and can't change nameservers; Tucows/Hover unknown.

## 2. One TXT record or two

Ours (`_fortropp-challenge.<hostname>`) proves ownership. Cloudflare for SaaS may also want its own ownership record and certificate validation.
- HTTP validation: automatic once DNS points at us, no extra record, but a certificate gap while DNS cuts over.
- TXT pre-validation: zero-downtime migration of an already-live site, at the cost of another record.

Product choice: simplest setup, or no downtime when migrating?

Settled 2026-10-22: HTTP validation only at launch; TXT pre-validation could come later as an optional path for migrating live sites. Requirement: the UI must make the certificate gap (usually minutes after DNS switches, HTTPS fails meanwhile) very clear, since missing behaviour the user expects is jarring. Exact wording and placement decided with the user when the domain UI is built.

## 3. When a hostname is registered with Cloudflare

Cloudflare for SaaS bills per custom hostname beyond an included number.
- At admission: fastest to go live, but costs for domains that never point at us.
- Once DNS points at us: cheaper and abuse-resistant, slower to go live.

Plus deregistration on removal, transfer and umbrella bump-out, and a cleanup job for strays.

Settled 2026-10-22: register at admission. Eating the cost of never-pointed domains is part of the business model (convenience); the per-account hostname caps keep it viable. Deregistration and a reconciling cleanup job still needed.

Settled 2026-10-25, how registration stays consistent (replaces the first cut, which called Cloudflare after committing, so a crash or aborted request in between lost the effect):
- Claiming and granting are separate, along the seam the code already has: every claim writes the `domains` row and contests others' umbrellas (`revoke_others`, pending claims included); only admission grants the tombstone (`record_claim`), the account's own umbrella and KV routing.
- Write-ahead states: an admitted claim goes `registering` (row + contest only) → register with Cloudflare → KV route → one transaction: `active`, `registered_at`, tombstone, own umbrella. Verify and reclaim go through `registering` the same way. Leaving admission goes through `removing` (removal, website deletion) or `demoting` (umbrella bump-out): stop routing, deregister, then delete the row or move it to `pending_verification`. The response waits for the final state.
- One `status` column is the whole state machine: stable `pending_verification`, `active`, `lost`; in-between `registering` (register → `active`, or row deleted if Cloudflare refuses), `removing` (deregister → row deleted), `demoting` (deregister → `pending_verification`). `lost` needs no Cloudflare step (the winner's row owns the registration). Constraints: `active` has `registered_at`; `pending_verification`, `registering`, `lost` don't (`removing` can come from `registering`, so it may or may not); `domains_one_holder` is unique over `registering`/`active`/`removing`/`demoting`. No timestamps for in-flight work: every driver step is idempotent and guarded by the row's status, so a request and a repair settling the same row at once is harmless. Edited into `0001_init.sql` (no live database yet).
- Grants on becoming `active` are derived from the row, not stored: proven apex → umbrella + restore of pending subdomains; unproven apex → umbrella, contested if another account claims under the root; subdomain → nothing; always the tombstone. KV routes are derived from the database (the hostname's `active` row; none → no route); a `registering` row's route is written before it is finalized; redirectors to a changed domain are re-derived after (a crash there leaves only a stale redirect in KV, reconciler territory). The driver settles every in-between row under the touched root domains. Activity events are written inside the transaction.
- Cloudflare errors: `4xx` other than `429` is permanent (undo the claim, return Cloudflare's reason); network, `5xx`, `429` are transient (row stays `registering`, label "Setting up").
- Settling runs as a spawned task the request awaits (a closed tab can't abort it), with retries after 1, 3 and 9 seconds for transient errors. Recovery: at startup (all in-between rows, in the background) and lazily when a website's domains are listed (its in-between rows, settled in the background so the page never waits). No periodic job, no age threshold.
- Checking the fake against reality: `infra/test-services` holds the fake as a library; `just cloudflare-contract <commit>` extracts our three operations from Cloudflare's official OpenAPI schema (`cloudflare/api-schemas`, pinned at `d04a1dd`) into `contract/cloudflare-custom-hostnames.json`; `cargo test` there validates every fake response against it. Found 2026-10-25: Cloudflare's `4XX` schemas for list and create combine the success and failure envelopes, so they reject every real failure; those are validated against the failure envelope alone, and a test fails when Cloudflare fixes it. The delete `200` schema is permissive, so the real-API probe stays the reference there; the probe also pins that a refusal is a `4xx` with code 1411.
- A permanent Cloudflare rejection (e.g. code 1411, or the hostname already a custom hostname at another Cloudflare customer) removes the claim through the removal path and returns the reason to the user.
- Separately, a pure reconciler (`plan(desired, actual)` → batched apply) catches drift from outside our events; only it batches. Writes in requests stay one at a time (they're rare).
- The KV `host:` records had the same commit-then-act gap; the driver now writes and deletes them as part of each transition. Changing a redirect still writes KV right after its commit (a small KV-only gap for the reconciler). Billing (item 12, with enforced caps) is a prerequisite for going live: production before billing is private testing only. Settled 2026-10-22: during private testing, signup is closed by a backend config flag with an email allowlist (not Cloudflare Access, which would block MCP/OAuth clients); removed or opened at launch.

## 4. The go-live gate and domain states

"Waiting for DNS → Issuing certificate → Live" (plus "Error"). Poll Cloudflare's custom-hostname and SSL status, or use its notifications? Which status counts as "serves"?

Settled 2026-10-22: five states: Awaiting proof (our DB, exists today), Waiting for DNS, Issuing certificate, Live, Error (the last four from Cloudflare's hostname and SSL status, Error with Cloudflare's message in plain words). Queried from Cloudflare on domains-page load plus a "Check" button during setup; never stored in our DB. The gate enforces itself (no HTTPS without a certificate), so the worker needs nothing new. Cloudflare's webhook notifications only if we later email "your domain is live".

Mapping settled 2026-10-25 against the real API (probe on `fortropp.app`): Waiting for DNS = hostname `pending` with certificate `initializing`/`pending_validation`, shown with Cloudflare's `verification_errors` as a hint (a fresh hostname reports "custom hostname does not CNAME to this zone." within seconds, which is normal, not an error); Issuing certificate = certificate `pending_issuance`/`pending_deployment`; Live = hostname `active` and certificate `active`; Error = only states that won't resolve on their own (`*_timed_out`, `blocked`, `moved`, deleted or missing at Cloudflare) plus an explicit unrecognised variant keeping the raw value. API facts: lookup by `?hostname.exact=` returns an empty list for unknown names; deleting a missing id returns error `1436` (treat as already gone); create with `ssl: {method: "http", type: "dv"}`; Cloudflare refuses `example.com/net/org` (error 1411). UI settled 2026-10-25, replacing today's plain "Admitted" label for active domains: **Waiting for DNS** (link-button like "Awaiting proof", opens the pointing step of the setup with Cloudflare's hint and a Check button; must look clickable so users know they can look deeper), **Issuing certificate** (link-button opening a short note: the domain reaches fortropp, securing it usually takes a few minutes, visitors may see a security warning meanwhile), **Live** (plain text), **Needs attention** (red link-button like "Verified elsewhere", opens a dialog with Cloudflare's reason as a "Could not …"-style sentence and what to try). The UI never labels a state "Error"; failures are sentences in red text or toasts. The backend uses its own token (SSL and Certificates write on `fortropp.app` only) and the zone id, both in `backend/.env` as `CLOUDFLARE_HOSTNAMES_TOKEN` / `CLOUDFLARE_HOSTNAMES_ZONE_ID`.

## 5. The preview wildcard

`<token>.preview.<domain>` is a second-level wildcard; Cloudflare's free Universal SSL covers only first-level wildcards (`*.example.com`). Either a paid certificate (Advanced Certificate Manager) or a flatter name, such as a dedicated preview domain where the token is the first label.

Settled 2026-10-22: flat prefix under the user-content domain, `preview-<token>.<user-content-domain>`, covered by the free first-level wildcard (Netlify and Vercel flatten previews into one label for the same reason). A separate preview domain was rejected as untrustworthy-looking; Advanced Certificate Manager ($10/month) bought only appearance. Site addresses (`<site-id>.<user-content-domain>`) never start with `preview-`, so the two can't collide. Code change: the worker parses `<token>.<PREVIEW_DOMAIN>` today (`serve-core/src/flow.rs:195`, settings in `src/platform.rs`, Rocq `Worker.v` `settings`/`parse_preview`); it becomes the user-content domain plus the `preview-` prefix, Rust and Rocq together, backend preview URLs to match.

## 6. Domain layout

User content lives on its own registrable domain, separate from the app's, so site JavaScript can never touch app cookies. Item 5 already speaks of a user-content domain; production makes it concrete: which domains, which Cloudflare zone, which is the Cloudflare for SaaS zone.

2026-10-22: `fortropp.dev` is owned (the app domain candidate; `.dev` is HSTS-preloaded, HTTPS only). `fortropp.com` and `fortropp.org` are held by dead sites, not wanted. Available per whois/RDAP that day: `fortropp.site`, `fortropp.host`, `fortropp.website`, `fortropp.net`, `fortropp.io`, `fortropp.app`, `fortropp.page`. Settled 2026-10-22: `fortropp.app` is the user-content domain (bought that day): the Cloudflare for SaaS zone, the edge name `edge.fortropp.app`, site addresses `<site-id>.fortropp.app`, previews `preview-<token>.fortropp.app`. `fortropp.dev` is the dashboard and API only, never user content, testing included (a catch-all worker route on `fortropp.dev` would swallow the dashboard). Public Suffix List (checked 2026-10-22 against publicsuffix.org/submit and the list's wiki Guidelines): `fortropp.app` would go in the PRIVATE section. Requirements: a `_psl` TXT record linking the PR, registration expiring more than 2 years after the PR date, a rationale of general utility; "projects not serving more than thousands of users are quite likely to be declined", no review-time promises. So it is not a pre-launch task: submit once fortropp serves thousands of users, after extending the registration. Until then `*.fortropp.app` addresses share cookies at the browser level (custom domains are unaffected); users needing cookie isolation should use a custom domain. Settled 2026-10-22 as the whole fix (narrow exposure: only apps with logins on `fortropp.app` addresses, which don't exist yet beyond previews; temporary once the PSL entry lands): one sentence in the docs recommending a custom domain, or `__Host-` cookies with CSRF tokens, for sites with logins, worded as general security best practice, not as a limitation of fortropp (user, 2026-10-22: don't turn people off). Nothing in the worker or `fortropp.toml`.

Launch blocker found 2026-10-22 (from reading the code, to confirm with a test first): nothing stops a user claiming our own domains. `Domain::claim` (`backend/src/models/domain.rs:306`) has no deny-list and `RegistrableHostname` (`worker/site-config/src/hostname.rs:56`) only rejects public suffixes. Claiming `fortropp.app` (an apex, admitted without proof) would route it, and the umbrella would then admit other users' `<site-id>.`, `preview-` and `edge.` names free. Fix: reserve `fortropp.app`, `fortropp.dev` and everything under them, refused at parse or claim. The PSL entry doesn't fix this, it makes subdomains worse: once `fortropp.app` is a public suffix, every `x.fortropp.app` is an apex, admitted without proof. The reservation must hold before and after the PSL change (the `psl` crate bundles the list at build time).

Settled 2026-10-22, config and defence in depth: a new `USER_CONTENT_DOMAIN` setting (`fortropp.app`; `fortropp.test` in dev/test) in both backend and worker, replacing the worker's `PREVIEW_DOMAIN`. Backend: reserved = `USER_CONTENT_DOMAIN` and `FRONTEND_ORIGIN`'s host, each with everything under them, matched on the full hostname (never the PSL root), refused with a plain error. Worker: a host under `USER_CONTENT_DOMAIN` is routed by shape only (`preview-<token>.` now, `<site-id>.` later), never through a `host:` lookup, anything else under it 404s; a Rocq law states it. Built together with the `preview-` change. First step: a failing e2e test claiming `fortropp.test` and `x.fortropp.test`.

Settled 2026-10-28: **Postmark, over its HTTP API** (not SMTP). Compared on paid plans, since the free tiers only last until launch: Postmark Basic $15/month for 10k emails covers transactional and broadcast; Resend $20 (50k) plus a marketing plan from $40/month past 1k contacts. Resend's built-in contact lists bought nothing: CASL makes us record consent (when, how) in our own database anyway (marketing opt-in plan, `todo_list.md` Misc), so the list lives with us; Postmark's broadcast stream (separate infrastructure from transactional, so campaigns can't hurt sign-in mail) handles unsubscribe links and suppression, synced back by webhook. Cloudflare Email Service (public beta, transactional only) and Amazon SES (no list or unsubscribe tooling, sandbox, own reputation) rejected. Why the API: Postmark's SMTP endpoint accepts every message and reports failures later (bounce webhook), so a password reset to a suppressed address would show "check your email" while nothing is sent; the API answers at once (`406` inactive recipient), which keeps user-facing errors injective. Also HTTPS on 443 instead of relying on port 2525 staying open, and one request instead of an SMTP handshake per message. Dev and test: a fake Postmark in `infra/test-services` (like the fake Cloudflare) accepting Postmark's `/email` requests and delivering into Mailpit, so tests keep reading mail there; Postmark publishes no API schema found so far, so the backstop is a hand-run real-API test (`POSTMARK_API_TEST` token validates without sending). Postmark reviews new accounts before they can send freely, and expects opt-in lists for broadcasts.

Settled 2026-10-28, suppressed recipients: Postmark answers HTTP `422` with `ErrorCode` 406 ("Inactive recipient") when the address is on the stream's suppression list (suppressions are per stream, so a marketing unsubscribe never blocks transactional mail). On a 406 the backend asks the Suppressions API for that address's record and reports the actual reason, never a guess: `HardBounce` (bounced as undeliverable: check the address or use another), `SpamComplaint` (reported as spam, can't send anymore; Postmark can't lift it, so don't suggest otherwise), `ManualSuppression` (only we set it on the transactional stream: contact support). Shown where the address is the user's own input and no account is revealed (signup; a future change-email page). Forgot-password stays silent: it answers the same whether an account exists, so a suppression there is logged (incident reference) and the response is unchanged; watch the logs. Not decided yet: reactivating a `HardBounce` (deleting the suppression) from the UI.

Settled 2026-10-28, forgot-password decoys (user's design): when no account has the address, the backend sends a decoy email to Postmark's documented discard address `test@blackhole.postmarkapp.com` on the same transactional stream, so both paths make the same Postmark call. This removes two oracles: send failures (Postmark down, rate limited, bad token) now happen for any address, so they can be shown to the user as real errors; and the timing difference (today an unknown address returns without calling Postmark). It also leaves an audit trail in Postmark's activity. Rule that keeps it oracle-free: only recipient-independent failures are shown (outage, `429`, authentication); recipient-dependent ones (`406` suppression, `300` Postmark refusing an address our parser accepted) can't happen to the decoy, so on the real path they stay silent and are logged with an incident reference. Cost accepted: every forgot-password request is a billable send (blackhole sends count toward the quota), so rate limits on it are a launch requirement (item 13).

## Why the domain rules are shaped this way

- **The threat is unverified claims:** squatting, and dangling-DNS takeover after a delete or domain change. A spoofed `Host` only gets another fortropp site's content under our address.
- **Proof is one rule:** a TXT token at `_fortropp-challenge.<full hostname>`, as a real TXT at that exact name. Proving one name proves nothing about its parent or siblings, and a wildcard can't open anyone's gate.
- **Subdomains are always verified, because conflict checks depend on order.** An attacker could claim `a.b.com` before anything under `b.com` exists, and go live when the owner later adds a `*.b.com` wildcard.
- **A contest covers the whole root, not just the bare name.** An owner may only ever use `shop.a.com` plus a wildcard, so an attacker's free `promo.a.com` would otherwise never be unseated. Accepted: a bogus claim can force the holder to prove subdomains once. Remaining gap: an owner adding wildcard DNS before any fortropp claim, while an attacker already holds a name there.
- **Tombstones never expire,** because dangling CNAMEs last for years. Passing verification still overrides them, because domains get sold.
- **Hostname caps are per account, not per site,** because sites multiply and each hostname costs money at Cloudflare.
- **Point by hostname, not A record,** so IPs can move without customers changing DNS.

## Why the rate limits are these

- **Each limit is tied to a failure:** emails per address 5/hour (inbox flooding, billable sends); failed sign-ins per typed address 10/10 min (distributed guessing, counted whether or not an account exists, so a pause reveals nothing); domain Check 30/min (DNS and Cloudflare calls); API/MCP token 600/min (overloading Postgres or R2); OAuth `/authorize` per IP 10/min; OAuth `/token` per grant 10/min, never per IP, since hosted clients share egress IPs.
- **Product caps aren't Redis counters.** They're facts about what an account holds, counted in Postgres in the transaction that adds the resource.
- **Turnstile only guards the three human forms, and fails closed.** No Bot Fight Mode, which would break MCP, OAuth agents and CI.
- **Accepted edge:** an attacker can pause an address and exhaust its email cap together, leaving up to 30 minutes with no working reset link. Loosening a limit for deliberate abuse was rejected, and exempting reset emails would buy unlimited billable sends.
